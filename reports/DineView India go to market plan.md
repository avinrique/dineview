# Sell the dish, not the QR code

DineView should launch in India as a per-outlet subscription priced on what no Indian rival sells, real-size 3D/AR dishes and per-dish nutrition, plus a one-time 3D capture fee. It should be sold neighbourhood by neighbourhood to Bengaluru's premium casual-dining restaurants, cafés and breweries, with FSSAI calorie display as the way into chains. The founders' plan has the right shape: 60 paying outlets, about ₹1.08 lakh MRR and about 800 dishes in 3D by April 2027, on about ₹16 lakh [plan]. The research notes back its choice of city, channels and expansion order. Bengaluru seated more Swiggy Dineout diners than any other Indian city in 2025, and its restaurant associations spent mid-2026 fighting 8–28% aggregator commissions. Petpooja already bundles QR ordering, a kitchen display and waiter calls into its ₹20,000–30,000 tiers, so DineView's premium has to come from the dish, not the QR code. Four numbers should change. The 3D pack at ₹1,000 a dish and extras at ₹900 [plan] sit at or below the notes' estimated production cost of ₹600–2,800 a dish [estimate], and should rise to about ₹1,500 [suggested]. Churn should be planned at 3–5% a month [suggested], not the 2.5% [plan] that counts only restaurant closures. The ₹3–4 lakh cleanup budget [plan] covers 800 dishes only if each needs about 30 minutes or less [estimate]. And 8 of 10 pilots paying by January [plan] is too early. Evidence that AR changes orders is thin and mostly comes from vendors: the best-known result, 41% versus 18% dessert orders, came from a study co-run by an AR vendor. India's own trial found calorie labels cut calories ordered by 10–12%. That makes DineView's table-level A/B pilot both its sales tool and the category's first independent proof. After Bengaluru, the notes support the founders' order: four Indian metros, then Dubai as the overseas base, then Saudi Arabia, where every menu, digital ones included, has had to show calories and allergens since July 2025.

*How the numbers are labelled: [plan] marks the founders' operating assumptions; [estimate] marks figures worked out from cited inputs, either here or in the research notes; [suggested] marks changes this report recommends. Every other figure links to its source.*

## Bengaluru has the most dine-out diners in India, so start there

Online delivery was only **about 11% of Indian food-service spend in FY26**. Organised operators, the ones who buy software, are growing 17–18% a year, against 3–4% for the unorganised segment ([Redseer](https://redseer.com/digests/india-food-services-market-growth-profitability-2030/)). Bengaluru is not the largest market by value. Mumbai, valued at about ₹55,181 crore in NRAI's IFSR 2024 Mumbai chapter, leads the organised segment ([Curly Tales](https://curlytales.com/indian-food-services-industry-ranks-3rd-largest-in-india-reveals-report-mumbai-food-services-industry-estimated-to-be-valued-at-%e2%82%b955181-crores/amp/)). But Bengaluru leads on the behaviour DineView sells into. It seated **4.5 million Swiggy Dineout diners from January to November 2025**, ahead of Delhi (3.9 million) and Hyderabad (3.7 million). It also led group bookings, and premium-dining bookings there grew more than 120% ([Swiggy](https://www.swiggy.com/corporate/press-release/how-india-swiggyd-2025-a-feast-for-the-fast-the-foodie-and-the-future/)). It took about a third of F&B leasing across seven cities and 40% of new pubs and breweries, with Indiranagar the preferred high street ([JLL via The Realty Today](https://therealtytoday.com/news/market-insights/indias-retail-leasing-soars-as-fb-sector-drives-4-million-sq-ft-jll-report/)). It has more than 70 microbreweries ([Beer Asia](https://beerasia.net/?p=3283)). City hotel associations count **about 34,000 eateries, nearly 20,000 of them on delivery apps** ([South First](https://thesouthfirst.com/karnataka/bengaluru-eateries-threaten-to-boycott-swiggy-zomato-over-unfair-business-practices/)). No source counts the premium and casual-dining subset. The notes put it in the low thousands [estimate], still dozens of times the 60-outlet target. A table for two at trending venues spends ₹1,200–3,000 ([Swiggy Dineout](https://blog.swiggy.com/dineout/trending-restaurants-in-bangalore-for-dineout/)), so a 5–10% lift is worth ₹75–300 a table [estimate]. That per-table gain is what the subscription has to beat.

The timing is unusually good. On 29 July 2026 the Bruhat Bengaluru Hotels Association (BBHA) threatened to pull its members off Swiggy and Zomato from 15 August over **commissions of 8–28%**. Its president, S. Subramanya Holla, said menu prices might have to rise by as much as 43% for a restaurant to net ₹100 ([OneIndia](https://www.oneindia.com/india/will-bengaluru-restaurants-pull-the-plug-on-swiggy-and-zomato-after-august-15-8160433.html)). One estimate of the fee stack leaves the restaurant about ₹220 of a ₹400 dish ([The Week](https://www.theweek.in/news/biz-tech/2026/07/30/wont-take-orders-anymore-why-bengaluru-restaurants-are-threatening-to-boycott-zomato-swiggy.html)). More than 1,000 owners backed the boycott ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/amp/india/story/bengaluru-restaurants-to-boycott-swiggy-zomato-from-august-15-heres-why-545884-2026-07-29)). The platforms agreed to ask consent before running discounts, and the deadline moved to 1 September ([Angel One](https://www.angelone.in/news/market-updates/swiggy-zomato-agree-to-consent-based-discounts-bengaluru-hotel-bodies-defer-boycott-to-september-1-2026)). The notes did not find how the dispute ended. Either way, this audience will easily understand a commission-free tool that grows the dine-in bill they keep in full. Diners point the same way. Bengaluru led Swiggy's "High Protein" category, which had 23 million orders nationally in 2025 ([Swiggy](https://www.swiggy.com/corporate/press-release/how-india-swiggyd-2025-a-feast-for-the-fast-the-foodie-and-the-future/)), and that suits the Nutrition button. Gen Z influences 48% of Indian dining-out spend, and 77% of Gen Z say they find AR and interactive visuals more engaging ([Snap–BCG via PPC Land](https://ppc.land/gen-z-to-drive-2-trillion-in-indian-consumer-spending-by-2035/)).

**QR ordering itself has no price premium left.** Petpooja claims more than 100,000 restaurants and raised ₹137 crore in September 2025 ([Entrackr](https://entrackr.com/news/petpooja-raises-155-mn-series-c-at-over-3x-valuation-premium-10512396)). It bundles Scan & QR Order, a kitchen display, a captain app and waiter calling into its Operations Manager tiers at ₹20,000 and ₹30,000. Its rupee pricing page does not say what period that covers. Petpooja switched to annual-only billing in July 2026, which suggests the prices are per outlet per year ([Petpooja](https://www.petpooja.com/pricing); [PricingSaaS](https://pricingsaas.com/companies/petpooja)). Abroad, standalone QR-ordering companies have been absorbed or have shrunk. Mr Yum merged with me&u ([Business News Australia](https://www.businessnewsaustralia.com/articles/hospitality-platforms-mr-yum-and-me-u-merging-to-become-a-new-global-giant.html)). Sunday pulled out of 60% of its markets ([Restaurant Dive](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/restaurant-payments-app-sunday-cuts-staff-exits-60-of-its-markets/627312)). DoorDash bought Bbot ([Restaurant Dive](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/doordash-buys-bbot-to-bolster-in-store-digital-ordering-capabilities/619559/)).

**The gap is the dish itself.** None of the Indian players reviewed shows 3D/AR dishes or per-dish nutrition on the table menu. Petpooja describes AR as an add-on layer that sits on top of its POS, not something it builds ([Petpooja AR glossary](https://blog.petpooja.com/glossary/ar-menu/)). The first wave of AR menus stalled on two problems. QReal, the company that grew out of Kabaq, blamed app downloads and the lack of 3D models for slow adoption ([The Spoon](https://thespoon.tech/?p=24480)). It then drifted into e-commerce 3D work and was divested ([Glimpse 10-K via TradingView](https://tr.tradingview.com/news/tradingview%3A0d7a68167935d%3A0-glimpse-group-inc-sec-10-k-report)). India's only funded AR-menu startup, peAR, used a native app and charged commission. It raised ₹6.7 crore across its disclosed rounds and has had no news since 2023 ([YourStory](https://yourstory.com/2021/07/deep-tech-startup-pear-pre-seriesa-inflection-ventures/amp); [IndiaRetailing](https://www.indiaretailing.com/2023/03/20/in-restaurant-ordering-app-pear-raises-rs-4-2-crore-funding/)). DineView's no-install WebAR removes the first problem. Its capture operation has to solve the second.

That sets the positioning: DineView is the menu layer that shows the dish, and it plugs into the restaurant's POS. Sell it first to premium casual dining, breweries and dessert-led cafés, and to chains for compliance. Present it as helping waiters sell, not as replacing them. Crew turnover runs 70–80% a year ([Petpooja](https://blog.petpooja.com/operations-workflows/5-easy-strategies-to-retain-your-restaurant-staff/)). But Petpooja's own guide warns that QR ordering still needs at least one waiter and takes upselling away from fine-dining captains ([Petpooja](https://blog.petpooja.com/industry-business-guides/what-is-scan-and-order-qr-ordering-indian-restaurants/)).

## Price 3D capture above cost and show AR in every tier

The model has two revenue lines: a per-outlet subscription and a one-time 3D capture fee. The restaurant stays the merchant for every diner payment. The founders' prices hold up well against the market. The capture fee is the exception: it is set at or below DineView's own likely cost.

**The capture fee is the plan's biggest error.** The notes build a per-dish cost from three parts [estimate]:

| Cost part | Basis | Per dish |
|---|---|---|
| Capture labour | Associate paid ₹25,000–35,000 a month, capturing 200–300 dishes a month | ₹100–175 |
| Freelance cleanup | 0.5–1.5 hours at the $12–20 an hour Indian Blender freelancers list ([Fiverr](https://www.fiverr.com/radanimate); [Hubstaff Talent](https://hubstafftalent.net/profiles/asad-sayyed)), at an assumed ₹85 to the dollar | ₹500–2,550 |
| Tools | — | under ₹50 |

That adds up to **₹600–2,800 a dish** [estimate]. A price of ₹1,000 loses money everywhere except the bottom of that range, and ₹900 extras lose money even at the plan's own ceiling. A price of ₹1,500 is still the bottom of the Indian quote range, so the "cheaper than vendors" message survives. It also keeps DineView well under the market's total first-year cost. The Restaurant tier paid annually plus 15 dishes at ₹1,500 comes to about ₹52,500 [estimate]. Petpooja's worked example for an AR menu comes to ₹88,560 for 12 models, standees and a platform fee, with no ordering or kitchen display included ([Petpooja AR glossary](https://blog.petpooja.com/glossary/ar-menu/)). Capture fees are also the bigger source of cash in the first six months. At the plan's prices they bring in about ₹6–7 lakh, against about ₹2 lakh of SaaS revenue on monthly billing [estimate]. Set the final per-dish price once the first three shoots have measured the real cost.

**Give the Café tier AR, and add a "menu layer" option for Petpooja outlets.** AR is the one feature no Indian competitor sells. Once a dish model exists, AR costs nothing extra, because the same GLB and USDZ files drive both the 3D view and the table view. The only measured AR effect is on desserts, which the notes tie most closely to dessert-led cafés. Without AR, the Café tier is a 3D QR menu competing with a commodity. Separate the tiers on dish count, waiter calls, analytics and POS sync instead. Many outlets already pay Petpooja for scan-and-order and a kitchen display. For them, offer AR and nutrition with Petpooja sync and no duplicate ordering stack. The notes judge a POS-integrated menu product easier to sell than a parallel ordering stack, though owners still need to confirm this. Two groups remain the natural buyers of DineView's own ordering and kitchen display: outlets on Petpooja's Android POS, which does not support Scan & Order, and outlets with no POS at all ([Petpooja](https://blog.petpooja.com/industry-business-guides/what-is-scan-and-order-qr-ordering-indian-restaurants/)).

**The chain tier needs three adjustments.** FSSAI's display rule covers establishments with a central licence or outlets at 10 or more locations. They must show calories per serving with serving size, a 2,000 kcal reference statement, allergens and the veg/non-veg logo, within a 25% tolerance ([S.S. Rana](https://ssrana.in/?p=14156)). An April 2024 clarification lets restaurants choose how to display them ([Lexplosion](https://lexplosion.in/fssai-re-iterates-requirement-for-e-commerce-food-aggregators-to-obtain-nutritional-information-from-food-business-operators-and-display-on-their-platforms/)):

| Information | Where FSSAI allows it |
|---|---|
| Calories | Menu cards, boards or booklets |
| Allergens and the veg/non-veg logo | Menu cards or boards |
| Full nutrition | Booklets, handouts or a website |

The notes found nothing confirming that a QR menu on its own counts as a "menu card or board". So the chain product should show kcal and allergen icons on each swipe card rather than behind the Nutrition button. It should also export a printable compliant menu, and feed the same data to Swiggy and Zomato, which must display it too. Open the tier to multi-state operators with fewer than 10 outlets as well, since a vendor reading says a central licence alone triggers the rule ([Petpooja](https://blog.petpooja.com/industry-business-guides/multi-outlet-menu-management-update-every-outlet/)). Enforcement on record is light: about 15–16 licences were suspended in 2022 ([Agron](https://agronfoodprocessing.com/fssai-suspended-15-food-business-operators-licences-for-not-displaying-nutritional-value/)), and the notes found no 2025–26 enforcement data. The liability is real, though: misbranding fines reach ₹3 lakh ([LiveLaw](https://www.livelaw.in/amp/columns/food-safety-and-standards-authority-of-india-fssai-food-safety-and-standards-act-2006-act-176134)). Contracts should therefore make the restaurant, as the food business operator, sign off its own nutrition data. The menu should show the restaurant's FSSAI licence number and state that food information comes from the restaurant.

**Billing follows Indian norms, with three snags.** The 10× annual price itself is standard: GoFrugal sells "pay for 10 months and use for a year" ([GoFrugal](https://www.gofrugal.com/pricing.html)), and Petpooja is now annual-only. The first snag is that UPI AutoPay debits up to ₹15,000 without a PIN after a 24-hour notice ([Stripe](https://docs.stripe.com/payments/upi/upi-autopay)), while larger recurring debits need a PIN every time ([Juspay](https://juspay.io/mandates/docs/how-to-integrate/enable-mandate-recurring-debits)). Monthly Café (₹1,769 with GST) and Restaurant (₹3,539) bills fit under the cap. Annual Café (₹17,688) and Restaurant (₹35,388) bills do not, and nor does a 10-outlet chain's monthly bill (₹23,588) [estimate]. Annual renewals and chains therefore need invoices paid by bank transfer, card or e-NACH. The second snag is that GST on the fee is probably a real cost to restaurants. SaaS carries 18% GST ([PayPro Global](https://payproglobal.com/saas-sales-tax/india/)), and NRAI is lobbying for a choice between 12% with input tax credit and 5% without ([CXO Digital Pulse](https://www.cxodigitalpulse.com/?p=19107)), which implies most restaurants cannot reclaim the 18% today. Quote GST-inclusive prices to owners, and have the CA confirm this in November.

The third snag is the payment flow itself, which DineView must stay out of. Diner payments should settle into each restaurant's own gateway or UPI account, with DineView invoicing its fees separately. This avoids two risks. Under GST section 9(5), an e-commerce operator for restaurant services owes 5% GST on the food bills, in cash and with no input credit ([CBIC Circular 167/23/2021](https://www.gstcouncil.gov.in/sites/default/files/2024-06/circular-167-2021-gst.pdf)). And a non-bank payment aggregator needs RBI authorisation and ₹15 crore of net worth ([MediaNama](https://www.medianama.com/2025/09/223-explained-rbi-master-direction-payment-aggregators/)). Razorpay Route, the split-payments product, now requires more than ₹40 lakh of turnover ([Razorpay](https://razorpay.com/docs/payments/route/)). Use Razorpay's partner programme instead, which onboards each restaurant as its own sub-merchant ([Razorpay](https://razorpay.com/docs/partners/aggregators/onboarding-api/)). Its revenue share is upside, not part of the base case.

**The ₹1,800 blended revenue figure [plan] is usable but depends on the tier mix.** Assume one 10-outlet chain among the 60 outlets. At monthly prices, ₹1,800 then needs about one standalone outlet in six on the Restaurant tier. If everyone prepays annually, it needs nearly half [estimate]. Breweries and premium casual dining, the notes' best-fit buyers, lean towards the Restaurant tier. Koramangala outlets and food-court counters lean towards Café. Track the mix weekly. Owners will compare the fee with their POS. Petpooja's revenue per client works out to roughly ₹7,700 a year [estimate], from FY24 revenue of ₹77.2 crore ([Inc42](https://inc42.com/company/petpooja/financials/)) spread over about 100,000 clients. So DineView has to sell as a revenue tool, and the pilot should report results in rupees per day. The Restaurant tier costs about ₹100 a day and the Café tier about ₹50 [estimate].

The plan's CAC range is the notes' estimate of rep cost and nothing else. The plan's own acquisition lines total ₹5.6 lakh: reps ₹3.6 lakh, creators ₹1 lakh and pilot costs ₹1 lakh [plan]. Spread over 60 outlets, that is about **₹9,300 per outlet** [estimate]. Free capture for the pilots and unpriced founder selling time push it higher. Even so, payback comes inside a year, and LTV:CAC beats the 2.5–3× one consultancy reports for Indian B2B SaaS ([upGrowth](https://upgrowth.in/?p=58566)).

Churn is the weaker assumption. The 2.5% a month converts one US finding to a monthly rate: 26% of new restaurants failed in their first year, falling to 19% in year two and 14% in year three. US census data puts first-year failure for independents lower, at 17% ([Petpooja, citing Parsa and BLS](https://blog.petpooja.com/?p=23372)). India publishes no closure rate at all. Two consequences follow. First, plan total churn at 3–5% a month, because unhappy customers leave as well as closed ones. Second, prospect restaurants that have been open more than a year. That cuts closure risk more than annual prepay does, since a prepaid restaurant that shuts simply does not renew.

### The A/B pilot can prove attach rates, not whole-bill uplift

The pilot is where the plan earns its evidence, and the notes' arithmetic shows what it can prove [estimate]:

| Metric | Bills needed per arm | Time at one outlet |
|---|---|---|
| Whole-bill AOV, 5% lift (assuming bill variation of 0.6) | about 2,260 | about 75 nights at 30 bills an arm |
| Dessert attach rate, 18% to 30% | about 195 | 1–2 weeks at a busy outlet |
| Dessert attach rate, Oxford-sized effect (18% to 41%) | about 58 | a few days |

Whole-bill AOV is too noisy to prove at one outlet; item attach rates are not. Run each pilot in two stages [suggested, following the notes]. First, two weeks of baseline with DineView ordering and AR off. Then alternate tables, or days, with AR on and off. The primary metric is the attach rate for 3–5 hero items per outlet (desserts, drinks, premium mains). AOV is secondary and should be pooled across outlets. Two refinements matter. Keep the Nutrition and Ingredients buttons on in both arms and switch only AR. India's ICMR trial found nutrition labels cut calories ordered by 10–12% at one chain ([The South First](https://thesouthfirst.com/health/do-menu-labels-make-diners-choose-fewer-calories-icmr-trial-in-chennai-says-yes-by-12/)), so switching both together would let one effect hide the other. And replace the assumed bill variation with each outlet's actual data in week one.

### Two milestones are too early, and April needs more than two reps

The case for DineView's core feature rests on one striking number and a handful of careful studies of intentions. The Oxford result is the only one that measured what diners actually ordered. It came from a study co-run with the AR vendor and gave no sample size, and even the article reporting it said it did not prove AR raises sales. The peer-reviewed work consistently finds that AR makes served food more vivid and raises intention to visit, share and buy. None of it measured real restaurant sales. Every percentage lift on a vendor website, from Kabaq's 20–26% to ARmenu's 2.4×, is unaudited marketing. And QReal, the best-known AR-menu company, left menus for e-commerce 3D work, where the budgets were. DineView can tell owners three things honestly: AR makes diners more engaged and more willing to share; in one vendor-run study it more than doubled dessert orders; and DineView will measure the effect at their tables, on their hero dishes, and report it in rupees. It cannot claim that AR guarantees a higher bill.

The nutrition side points the other way. India's best evidence on menu information, the ICMR trial, shows labels moving diners *towards fewer calories*, and Abu Dhabi's study agrees. The Fiji experiments suggest AR can also reduce over-ordering by making portion size clear. So the two halves of the product push orders in opposite directions. Sell nutrition as trust, health and compliance, never as a revenue feature, and sell AR as the attach-rate driver. That split suits Bengaluru, which leads India's protein ordering. It is also why the pilot design matters so much. If DineView publishes the method, sample sizes and confidence intervals of an AR-on versus AR-off test across ten Bengaluru outlets, it will hold the first independent evidence in a category that has run on vendor claims for a decade.

Most risks already have an answer in the plan, but three do not. The first is capture economics: this is where earlier AR menus struggled, and the plan prices capture at or below cost. The second is nutrition data, which has no owner and no budget line. The third is DineView's legal role for cross-restaurant data. For diner data a restaurant collects, the restaurant is most likely the data fiduciary and DineView the processor, and the DPDP Rules require security clauses in that contract. If DineView reuses diner data across restaurants for its own analytics or marketing, it becomes a fiduciary in its own right, with its own notice and consent duties. That conflicts with any investor story about a "data moat" built on personal data. Keep cross-restaurant analytics at dish level and free of personal data.

**Prepare the product now [suggested].** Three changes cost little in India and remove most of the Gulf engineering later. Store nutrition data to SFDA's stricter standard: 14 allergens, where FSSAI's list is shorter, plus sodium, caffeine and activity-equivalent fields. And work out how fees collected in the Gulf reach India, which the notes did not research. The notes put the Indian-metro step at about twelve months, which places a Dubai entity decision around late 2027 at the earliest [estimate].

## Conclusion

For its first six months, DineView is a 3D content business with a software subscription attached. Capture fees are the larger cash line, and capture cost is the variable most likely to break the plan. It is also the cost that held back earlier AR-menu companies. So the most important data DineView will collect in November is not a sales figure. It is the time and cost of its first three shoots, and pricing should follow that number rather than a competitor's quote.

The product's two halves pull diners in opposite directions. AR on the table is the one feature with any evidence, however weak, of selling more desserts. India's own trial shows nutrition labels steering diners towards fewer calories. That split shapes the pitch (AR earns revenue; nutrition earns trust and compliance) and the experiment (switch AR alone). The pull of regulation also grows with distance from Bengaluru: optional for standalone restaurants, mandatory for Indian chains and universal in Saudi Arabia. So the asset that travels furthest is not the outlet count. It is the per-dish nutrition and allergen database built during the Bengaluru pilots, provided it is built to the strictest standard from the start.
